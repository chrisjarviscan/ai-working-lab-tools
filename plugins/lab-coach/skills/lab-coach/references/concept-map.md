# Concept map: what to teach, and when

Each entry has a trigger and a plain explanation. Teach the concept only when its
trigger happens in the person's own work, or when they ask about it. Use your own words
and their example; the lines below show the size and tone, not a script.

Order does not matter. Most people will meet the first group early and the last group
late or never. A concept that never comes up never gets taught.

## Working with the assistant

**Context**
- When: the first result is generic, or wrong in a way that shows you lacked background.
- Say: "That came out bland because I only know what's in this conversation. Tell me who
  reads this and what happened last year, and watch what changes."
- Then: rebuild with their background and compare the two.

**Feedback instead of starting over**
- When: they accept a so-so draft, or throw it out and retype the whole request.
- Say: "You don't have to start again. Tell me what's off, the way you'd tell a
  colleague, and I'll fix just that."

**Examples beat descriptions**
- When: they struggle to describe a tone or format ("warmer, but professional").
- Say: "Paste one you liked. I can match an example much better than a description."

**One job at a time**
- When: a big request comes back muddled.
- Say: "I did three jobs at once and did each one half well. Let's split it."

**Long conversations fade**
- When: you lose track of something they told you earlier, or the chat has run very long.
- Say: "This conversation is long enough that I'm losing the early parts. Let's save lab
  notes and start a fresh one."

## Trusting the result

**Made-up facts**
- When: your draft contains a number, quote, name, date, or citation you cannot point to
  a source for, or they ask you for facts.
- Say: "Check that figure before you use it. I can produce something that sounds right
  and isn't. Anything that looks like a fact needs a source you can open."
- Then: mark what needs checking. If they do research across several places, the
  source-accountability tool in this repo is the next step.

**What I know has a date on it**
- When: they ask about something recent, or a deadline, rule, or price that changes.
- Say: "What I learned stops at a certain point, so I can be out of date. For this one,
  go to the funder's own page."

**Machine-sounding writing**
- When: they say a draft "sounds like AI," or a reader says so.
- Say: name the two or three specific habits in that draft. The anti-ai-slop tool in
  this repo checks for them.

**Private information**
- When: they are about to paste, or have pasted, details about clients, donors,
  volunteers, or staff.
- Say: "Before you paste that: does your organization have a rule about putting personal
  details into AI tools? If you're not sure, swap the names for placeholders for now and
  bring the question to the group session."
- Do not state what their plan or organization does with data. You do not know.

## Not repeating yourself

**Project**
- When: they paste the same background a second time, or come back for a second session
  on the same work.
- Say: "You've given me that background twice now. A project is a place to keep it, so
  every new conversation starts already knowing it."

**Skill**
- When: they have given the same instructions for the third time, or you have settled on
  steps that work and they will do this task again.
- Say: "We've now worked out how you like this done. A skill is those instructions
  saved, so I follow them every time without you retyping them."
- Then: draft the skill together from what they actually said in this conversation.

**Markdown file**
- When: they first open or save a skill or instruction file and see `.md` or the `#`
  symbols.
- Say: "That's a plain text file. The `#` marks a heading. That's all you need to know
  to edit it."

**Plugin**
- When: they have a skill and want to hand it to colleagues along with other pieces, or
  they are installing a set someone else made.
- Say: "A plugin is a bundle. It carries several skills, and sometimes other parts, so a
  colleague can install them in one step."

## Reaching their own material

**Connector**
- When: they copy and paste from their drive, email, or calendar into the chat, or ask
  whether you can see their files.
- Say: "You just copied that out of your drive by hand. A connector lets me look there
  myself, if your organization allows it."
- Then: say you can only use what is connected, and that availability depends on their
  plan and their organization's settings.

**A document that stands on its own**
- When: the result is something they will keep and edit, like a report or a tracker,
  rather than an answer in the chat.
- Say: "This wants to be its own document, not a chat message." Then make it one in
  whatever form their setup supports.

## Sharing and keeping things current

**GitHub and repos**
- When: they want a skill a colleague made, want to share theirs, or ask how the lab
  tools get updated.
- Say: "A repo is a shared folder with a history. The lab's tools live in one, so
  everyone gets the same version and can see what changed."

**The first people**
- When: the piece works for them and someone else does the same task.
- Say: go to part 4 of the build plan.

**Running without you**
- When: they ask whether this could happen on its own, on a schedule or in several
  steps.
- Say: "Sometimes, depending on your setup. It's a good one to bring to the group
  session, because the answer depends on what your organization has turned on."
