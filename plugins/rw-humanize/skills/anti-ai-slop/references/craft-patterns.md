# Layer 2: Craft Patterns (Patterns 5-12)

The surface layer asks "does this sound like a machine wrote it?" This layer asks
"does the piece work as writing?" A draft can pass every surface check and still
fail here. These are editorial-judgment patterns, not string matches, so they
need a full read, not a scan.

The eight patterns split into two groups. Three are missing ingredients: things
the text needs and lacks. Five are flaws: things the text does and should not.

## How to run this layer

1. Read the full piece once without flagging anything. You cannot judge structure
   from fragments.
2. Missing ingredients: walk paragraph by paragraph checking Patterns 5, 6, 7.
3. Flaws: walk again checking 8 through 12. Patterns 11 and 12 are piece-wide;
   they need counts and locations.
4. If rewriting, fix structure first (5, 6, 8), then tone (9, 10), then rhythm
   (11, 12). Structural fixes move paragraph boundaries, so rhythm work done
   first gets wasted.

## Missing ingredients (Patterns 5-7)

### Pattern 5: Cold Transitions / Missing Bridge Sentences

A paragraph opens on a new idea with nothing connecting it to the one before. The
reader does the transition work the writer skipped. Common in AI drafts because
models generate paragraphs as units and rarely look back.

Spot it: read only the last sentence of each paragraph and the first of the next.
If you cannot say how the second follows from the first, the bridge is missing.

Fix: add a sentence at the seam that carries one idea across it. The bridge names
the relationship: consequence, contrast, zoom-in, or example.

### Pattern 6: Compressed Structure

Ideas that each need room are crushed into one dense block. A paragraph makes a
claim, gives the mechanism, and draws the implication in three sentences, and the
reader retains none of it. The single most common failure in AI-drafted thought
leadership: the model knows the points but not their weight.

Spot it: a paragraph that reads like an outline. Count distinct ideas; three or
more that each deserve development means it is compressed.

Fix: split. Give each load-bearing idea its own paragraph with development: an
example, a consequence, a beat of acknowledgment. Mix brief paragraphs (30-80
words) with longer ones (100-180). Unfold ideas rather than stacking them.

### Pattern 7: Missing "What It Looks Like In Practice" Beat

A principle or framework stated without the concrete operational picture that
shows it working. The reader agrees in the abstract and has no idea what to do
Monday morning.

Spot it: after any claim, ask "what would I see if this were happening?" If the
piece never shows it, the beat is missing. Warning signs: a full section with no
named actor doing a named thing.

Fix: add the beat, with a specific actor, action, and setting. One vivid instance
beats three generic ones. Do not fabricate: if no real example exists, construct
an explicitly hypothetical one ("imagine a site leader who...") or name the gap.

## Flaws to remove (Patterns 8-12)

### Pattern 8: Protagonist Drift

The piece changes who it is about partway through. It opens inside the CSR
manager's experience, becomes about "companies," then "the sector," and by the
close the person it was written for has disappeared.

Spot it: mark the subject of each section (who acts, decides, struggles). If it
changes without acknowledgment, that is drift. Abstract nouns replacing a human
subject is the usual mechanism.

Fix: pick the protagonist and hold them. Other actors can appear, but the camera
returns. If the piece must move from individual to system, make the move explicit
and bring the protagonist along.

### Pattern 9: Empathy Not Sustained

The piece opens on the practitioner's side, then slides into judging them. The
person who was "doing their best inside a broken system" becomes the problem. The
reader who recognized themselves feels ambushed.

Spot it: track the emotional posture toward the practitioner. Watch for the turn:
"but too many managers simply...". Critique of intent or character rather than
framing is the tell.

Fix: rewrite critical passages from the practitioner's side. The system, the
incentives, the inherited playbook take the weight; the practitioner keeps their
dignity and gets a live choice. This is the craft-level twin of narrative Pattern
18 (flattened ambivalence).

### Pattern 10: Moralizing Vocabulary

Preaching instead of showing. The vocabulary of obligation and virtue: "should,"
"must," "we owe it to," "the right way," "it's time to," "we can no longer afford
to." The writer claims the moral high ground rather than earning agreement.

Spot it: search for obligation verbs and virtue framing. One "should" in an
operational sentence ("the brief should run ten minutes") is instruction, not
moralizing; "we should all be asking ourselves" is moralizing. The difference is
operational content versus moral posture.

Fix: replace obligation with consequence. Not "companies must stop counting
hours" but "hours tell you people showed up. They cannot tell you whether anyone
changed, and change is what the program was funded to produce." Let the reader
conclude the "should." This is the craft-level twin of narrative Pattern 13
(thematic over-determination).

### Pattern 11: Closing Thumps (piece-wide: count and locate)

Ending every section on a short dramatic punch line. "And that changes
everything." "This is the work." One thump can land. A thump at the bottom of
every section is a drum machine, and each devalues the rest.

Spot it: read only the final sentence of each section. Count the short, dramatic,
rhythmically identical ones. Two or more is a pattern; report count and all
locations.

Fix: keep at most one, at the moment of genuine highest stakes. Rewrite the
others to end on substance: the finding, the implication, the open question.

### Pattern 12: Contrastive Negation Overuse (piece-wide: count and locate)

"It's not X, it's Y" used repeatedly. Once per piece it can sharpen a
distinction. Repeated, it frames every idea as the correction of a strawman.

Spot it: search "not just," "isn't about," "it's not," "rather than," "less
about... more about." Count across the piece. This is the same construction the
surface layer's Family C caps at one per piece; the counts must agree.

Fix: keep the single strongest instance if it earns its place (never the final
line). Convert the rest to direct statements of what the thing is.

## Grounding

These patterns are grounded in the Chris Jarvis Voice contract: hold tension,
unfold rather than stack, anchor abstractions in examples, critique framing not
intent. Patterns 9 and 10 are the diagnostic side of the voice contract's ethical
posture. They apply in any voice; the examples lean RW because that is the home
context.
